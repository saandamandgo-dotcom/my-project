Introduction to Nanotechnology

Definition, scope, and fundamental concepts

What is Nanotechnology? Nanotechnology is the field of science that deals with the synthesis, management, and application of materials with a size smaller than 100nm.

A particle with at least one dimension of 1-100 nanometers (nm) is commonly called a nanoparticle (nanomaterial).

⚡Why Size Matters

The small particle size (or pore size) results in:

  • Large surface area or interfacial area
  • Greater chemical reactivity compared to bulk materials
  • Unique optical, electrical, and mechanical properties

Historical Perspectives

Pioneers who shaped nanotechnology

1857

Michael Faraday

Prepared aqueous colloidal blends containing gold nanoparticles less than 100nm. He discovered that these nanoparticles have exceptional optical and electrical properties that differ significantly from bulk gold.

Colloidal gold solutions showing different colors based on particle size
1925

Richard Zsigmondy - Nobel Prize

Measured the dimensions of nanoparticles such as gold colloids and used the nanoparticles concept for the first time. His work established the foundation for understanding colloidal systems.

Key Achievement: Developed the ultramicroscope and established methods for measuring particle sizes in colloidal systems.

Colloidal Systems

The bridge between bulk materials and nanomaterials

What are Colloidal Solutions? Colloidal solutions are mixtures where particles are dispersed uniformly throughout another substance. The particle size ranges between 1-1000 nm, making them the historical predecessor to modern nanotechnology.

💧Lyophilic Sols

(Solvent-loving)

  • High affinity for dispersion medium
  • Spontaneous tendency to form colloids
  • Reversible in nature
  • High viscosity
  • Lower surface tension
  • Examples: Starch, gelatin, proteins

⚡Lyophobic Sols

(Solvent-hating)

  • No affinity for dispersion medium
  • No spontaneous tendency to form colloids
  • Irreversible in nature
  • Unstable — needs electrolytes
  • Viscosity similar to medium
  • Particles carry positive or negative charge

Types of Colloidal Particles

🔬Multimolecular Colloids

Consist of a large number of atoms or smaller molecules aggregated together. Diameter is less than 1nm individually, but they aggregate to form colloidal size particles.

Usually lyophobic in nature. Example: Gold sol (aggregation of Au atoms).

🧬Macromolecular Colloids

Consist of big size molecules (macromolecules) which have sizes comparable to colloidal particles. The molecules themselves are in the colloidal range.

Usually lyophilic in nature. Examples: Proteins, starch, cellulose.

🎯Associated Colloids (Micelles)

Molecules contain both lyophilic and lyophobic parts. At low concentration, they behave as normal electrolytes. At high concentration, they form micelles — aggregated particles.

Figure: Micelle structure showing hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails

Normal Micelle

Hydrophilic heads face outward (aqueous solution), hydrophobic tails inward.

Reverse Micelle

Hydrophobic tails face outward (organic solvent), hydrophilic heads inward.

04

Properties of Colloids

Unique behaviors at the nanoscale

🎲1. Brownian Motion

The continuous, random movement of colloidal particles caused by collision with molecules of the dispersion medium.

Factors affecting intensity:
  • Particle size: Smaller particles → more vigorous motion
  • Viscosity: Less viscous medium → more vigorous motion
Figure: Brownian motion - random zigzag movement of particles

💡2. Tyndall Effect

When a strong beam of light passes through a colloidal sol, its path becomes visible due to scattering of light by colloidal particles.

Mechanism: Light passes through a colloid and is absorbed, transmitted, and scattered. The scattered light makes the beam path visible.
Figure: Tyndall effect demonstration showing light scattering in colloidal solution (right) vs true solution (left)

Lyophobic Colloids

Large difference in refractive indices → Well-defined Tyndall effect

Lyophilic Sols

Small difference in refractive indices → Weak Tyndall effect

Nanobiotechnology

Where biology meets nanotechnology

Interdisciplinary Field: Nanotechnology combines biology and physics to produce multifunctional systems and devices that are more sensitive, specialized, and efficient.

🧪Nanobiotechnology

Nanotechnology applied to biology

Using tools and techniques from nanotechnology to study biological systems or create devices for biological applications.

Example: Nanoparticles for Drug Delivery

Tiny nanoparticles (liposomes or dendrimers) carry drugs directly to cancer cells, minimizing side effects and improving efficacy.

🧬Bionanotechnology

Biology inspiring nanotechnology

Using biological principles to guide the development of nanotechnology. Learning from how biological systems work at the nanoscale.

Example: DNA Nanotechnology

Using DNA as a building block to create molecular machines, scaffolds for drug delivery, gene therapy, or nanoscale electronics.
